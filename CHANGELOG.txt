HAPPY TRAILS — CHANGELOG
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This history used to live at the top of beltline.html, because for a long time
that was the only finished page and every change landed on it. It does not live
there any more: beltline.html is generated from trails_data.py like every other
trail page, and a generated file is the wrong place to keep a record — the next
build throws it away.

So it is here, in the release folder, beside VERSION.txt. The numbering below is
the Beltline page's own, which is what the entries were written against; the
folder's version (site-assets-v1.x) is on VERSION.txt.

VERSION HISTORY
 18.3  EIGHT SMALLER THINGS, AND THE ABOUT PAGE AS IT WAS WRITTEN.

       ── THE ROW OF BUTTONS ON A TRAIL PAGE ───────────────────────────────

       ONE PAIR OF ARROWS, NOT TWO. Pressing play used to put a second back-
       and-forward pair on the screen, an inch from the pair that was already
       there, pointing the same way and very nearly doing the same job. The
       tour's line of words now joins the row that exists: it has no arrows of
       its own, and the three buttons that were already there drive the tour
       while it runs. Press play and "PRESS ESC TO LEAVE AUTOPLAY" appears
       along the row, to the right of the forward arrow.

       The press is caught before it reaches the button, in capture and on the
       document rather than on the button itself — a second listener on the
       button would have run AFTER the engine's, which stops the tour, so the
       tour would have ended a frame before it was told to go forward.

       WHERE YOU ARE, ALWAYS, beside those arrows: "4 / 13". It used to appear
       only while the tour was running, which is backwards — somebody being
       driven down the trail can see that something is happening, and somebody
       walking it themselves is the one with no idea whether there are two
       waypoints left or twenty. It is written from the same figures that grey
       the arrows out, so it cannot disagree with them, and it counts only the
       stops that are live: switching the Wishful layer off changes the total.

       IT CARRIES ITS OWN BACKGROUND, the arrows' own, because what is behind
       it is the map — pale here, dark there. Plain grey writing over that is
       legible over a ravine and gone completely over a lake.

       THE THREE BUTTONS ARE THE SAME SIZE. The play button was a sixth larger
       than its neighbours, which was meant to say "this is the one" and said
       "this row was not lined up on purpose" instead.

       ── AND WHAT THEY DO ─────────────────────────────────────────────────

       CLICKING A WAYPOINT ON THE ELEVATION GRAPH NO LONGER OPENS EVERY CARD
       ON THE WAY THERE. Going to a waypoint six stops down the trail means
       scrolling past five, and with auto-load on each of those opened its card
       and shut it again as it went by — half a second each, none of them the
       one asked for. It behaves like dragging the graph now: the cards are
       hushed for the length of the journey and the one asked for opens on
       arrival. Only when there is something in between, so stepping to the
       next waypoint still opens at once, as it always did.

       ── THE MAIN MAP ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────

       THE OPEN WAYPOINT IS MUCH HARDER TO LOSE. Its badge grows by nearly half
       again rather than the sixth it used to — which was the same amount a
       hover gives, so the open one was the same size as whatever the pointer
       happened to be resting on — and the ring that opens out of it KEEPS
       GOING for as long as its card is open, instead of flashing once. On the
       tour, where the card changes by itself every few seconds, "which of
       these am I reading?" is a question being asked continuously, so the
       answer is continuous too. Both rings are wider than they were.

       ── THE TWO INSTRUMENTS ──────────────────────────────────────────────

       THE DIAL'S NEEDLE. Reported three times now, and this time the cause is
       the real one: the animation has been running correctly all along, and
       nobody could see it because it ran BEHIND A TRANSPARENT CARD. A card
       does not appear, it fades in, over about half a second — and the page
       asked for the needle's swing at the moment it decided to show the card,
       which is the moment the fade begins. The needle therefore travelled all
       the way up from the bottom of the scale while there was nothing on
       screen; by the time there was, it had a fifth of its travel left and was
       moving about two degrees. Which looks exactly like a needle that does
       not move.

       The coin got away with the same thing because its turn is two full
       revolutions and its last fifth is still visibly a coin spinning. That is
       the whole of why one of them has been reported as broken and the other
       has not.

       Nothing is asked to move now until the card it is drawn on has actually
       faded in — measured, not guessed at — and the icons themselves refuse to
       start while they cannot be seen, so the same thing cannot happen again
       somewhere else.

       THE COMPASS ON A TRAIL PAGE ANSWERS THE POINTER, the way the one on the
       main map does. The rollover — the needle knocked off north, overshooting
       and settling — has been written and sitting in the stylesheet since the
       day the main map got it, and applied to the trail pages' badge as well.
       It could never fire, because the interface layer takes no pointer events
       at all and hands them out, and the badge had never been handed any. It
       has now.

       ── AND THE ABOUT PAGE ───────────────────────────────────────────────

       IT IS THE ONE THAT WAS WRITTEN, now, rather than the summary of it that
       was standing in: every section, in full, and all thirteen pictures. The
       two screenshots of the same corner of the cemetery sit side by side,
       because a comparison you have to scroll between is not a comparison.

 18.2  NINE CHANGES TO THE TOURS, THE MAP AND THE TWO READINGS.

       ── THE MAIN MAP ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────

       CLICKING THE MAP PUTS THE PALETTES AWAY, the way it already did on a
       trail page: a click anywhere that is not a pin, a route or a card closes
       the legend, the layers palette and any open waypoint. A CLICK AND DRAG
       DOES NOT — dragging the map is how you look around it, and having the
       panel you were reading vanish because you moved the map an inch is the
       kind of thing that makes a page feel like it is fighting you. The two are
       told apart by how far the pointer travelled between going down and coming
       up, not by which one fired first.

       THE FLIGHT TO THE FIRST WAYPOINT NO LONGER SWERVES. It used to slide the
       map's corner across the screen in a straight line while the zoom grew by
       a ratio each frame, and those two do not describe the same journey — the
       place you are flying to drifted sideways across the screen on the way in
       and was caught at the last moment, which read as a swerve. The flight now
       decides which POINT ON THE MAP has to sit under the middle of the screen,
       holds it there, and works the corner out from the zoom every frame. The
       target does not move at all now: it grows.

       THE TOUR IS FASTER, and the order of its stops IS SHUFFLED, so the map
       tour tells a different story each time it is started rather than reciting
       the same thirty-six in the same order. Shuffled properly (Fisher-Yates,
       every order equally likely) rather than by sorting on a random number,
       which is neither uniform nor, in some browsers, even stable.

       ── BOTH TOURS ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────

       ARROWS EITHER SIDE OF THE COUNT. "◀ 12 of 36 ▶" — the same note that
       tells you where you are now takes you a stop forward or back without
       leaving the tour. Pressing one cuts the current stop short rather than
       queueing behind it, so the tour follows the arrow immediately and picks
       its own pace up again from wherever you left it.

       EVERY SLIDE IN A CARD'S CAROUSEL GETS AN EQUAL SHARE of that card's time.
       The first one used to flash past: the code worked out when the LAST slide
       should begin and, on a card with a single page of text, that moment was
       zero — so every slide change fired at once and the carousel appeared to
       start on its second picture. Each slide now simply gets the card's dwell
       divided by the number of slides.

       ── THE TWO READINGS ─────────────────────────────────────────────────

       THE DIAL'S NEEDLE MOVES. It has not been moving, twice reported and twice
       "fixed", and the reason was one line of CSS: the keyframes worked the
       overshoot and the two settling wobbles out by multiplying one custom
       property by another inside a calc(), inside a keyframe, on an SVG
       element. Where that is not supported the whole keyframe is invalid and
       the needle sits still — no error, no warning, nothing to see. The four
       angles are worked out in JavaScript now and written down as plain
       degrees. The needle swings up from the bottom of the scale, goes a little
       past its reading, and settles back onto it.

       THE COINS ARE SHINY. A flare of brightness and contrast crosses each one
       as it turns edge-on and dulls as the face comes back round, which is what
       metal does under a light and what a flat disc rotating does not.

       ── AND ONE SMALL THING ──────────────────────────────────────────────

       THE GREEN DOT ON THE TRAIL GROWS INSTEAD OF DROPPING. Pointing at a
       waypoint made its dot slide downwards off the route — the hover rule
       replaced the transform that was holding the dot centred instead of adding
       to it. It scales up now, on the line, and the open waypoint's dot is
       larger still.

 18.1  FOUR THINGS PUT RIGHT, AND TEN ADDED.

       ── WHAT WAS WRONG ───────────────────────────────────────────────────

       THE WAYPOINT DOTS HAD COME OFF THE ELEVATION LINE. Making the thin strip
       easier to hit was done with padding, and padding was the wrong tool: the
       dots and the position knob are placed with a `top` in per cent, and a
       percentage measures itself against the PADDING box — so padding the
       graph silently pushed every dot off the line it belongs on. The reach is
       a pseudo-element now and the box nothing is measured against is
       untouched.

       THE CARD NO LONGER FLIES OUT OF ITS WAYPOINT. It was wrong twice over:
       the throw fought the rise the drawing loop was doing at the same moment,
       and because it ended on the transform the card had when it STARTED,
       clearing it let the card jump the last few pixels of that rise in one
       frame — a small hop a moment after the card had apparently finished
       arriving. That hop is gone with it. Cards arrive the way they always
       did.

       THE COIN AND THE DIAL NOW ACTUALLY MOVE on a trail page and on the main
       map. They were left to an observer that asks "has this come into view",
       which is right on the Wishful thinking page and useless here: every card
       on these pages is inside the viewport the whole time and simply faded to
       nothing, so the observer fired once, at load, while the card was
       invisible, and never again. The pages that fade their cards now ask for
       the animation at the moment a card becomes the one on screen.

       AND POINTING AT EITHER ONE PLAYS IT. A reading you have reached for is a
       reading you are asking about.

       ── THE TOUR ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

       TWICE AS FAST, and there is now one number that says so — `speed` at the
       top of autoplay.js. Every duration in the file is divided by it, so the
       pace is one lever and nothing else has to be touched. It is separate
       from `wordsPerMinute`, which is how fast a person READS and therefore
       how long a wordy card is compared with a short one: changing the first
       changes the shape of the tour, changing the second changes its speed.

       IT SWITCHES WAYPOINT AUTO-LOAD ON. With it off the walk opens nothing,
       so the tour would have scrolled the whole trail past a map with no cards
       on it.

       AND ANYTHING AT ALL STOPS IT — a waypoint clicked, a card closed, an
       arrow pressed, a panel opened, the graph dragged, the map clicked. The
       engine raises one word, `trail:handover`, from the handler of every real
       event a reader can cause, and the tour listens for it. It is raised
       where the BROWSER said a human was involved and never inside the
       functions themselves, which is why the tour — driving those same
       functions all day — does not stop itself. Anything added later that a
       person can press stops the tour by saying the same word.

       ONE CARD REPLACING ANOTHER IS NOW SOMETHING YOU CAN WATCH. It used to be
       instant: the outgoing card's opacity was set to zero in the same frame
       the incoming one appeared, and two things happening in one frame is not
       a change anybody can follow. The new card comes in from the right and
       SHOVES the old one out to the left, the old one stepping back a little
       as it goes.

       And the line saying how to leave the tour is solid, and has moved up off
       the elevation graph rather than sitting on it.

       ── AND TEN NEW THINGS ───────────────────────────────────────────────

       A LINK THAT STARTS THE TOUR.  beltline.html?play — so a trail can be
       handed to somebody as something to watch.

       A COUNT IN THE TOUR'S OWN LINE.  "4 of 13", from the stops that are
       actually live, so switching the wishful layer off changes the route and
       the total together.

       A TOUR OF THE WHOLE NETWORK, on the main map: a play button above the
       zoom controls that flies from one featured waypoint to the next,
       anywhere in the region. It shares exactly one thing with the trail
       pages' tour — how long a card is worth, which is in tour-pace.js and
       asked by both — because that is the only question on which the two must
       never disagree.

       THE ELEVATION GRAPH TAKES THE KEYBOARD.  It is a slider, so it behaves
       like one: left and right step a waypoint at a time, Home and End go to
       the ends. Which also means the whole trail can be walked without a
       mouse.

       THE GRADIENT UNDER YOUR HAND while the graph is being dragged — the one
       number a picture of a hill cannot give you, and the reason for dragging
       in the first place. Measured across a span rather than between two
       neighbouring samples, because a finely sampled profile is all noise.

       THE STEEP SECTIONS MARKED, in a warmer colour, drawn from the profile's
       own line so the mark lies exactly on it. Five per cent and up, which is
       about where a climb stops being scenery and starts being a decision.
       (Nothing is marked on a rail path, which is correct — they are built
       under two.)

       A LINK TO EACH WAYPOINT, on its card. Every waypoint has had an address
       of its own for a long time and nothing on the page ever offered it.

       WHERE YOU LEFT OFF. A trail is nine screenfuls; coming back to one and
       finding yourself at the trailhead again is a small tax on having stopped
       to do something else. A chip offers to put you back — once, from the top
       of the page only, and it takes silence for a no.

       THE NEXT WAYPOINT'S PICTURES, FETCHED while the current one is being
       read, so the tour does not arrive at a grey rectangle. Off on a metered
       connection.

       AND WHAT IS NEW ON THE WISHFUL THINKING PAGE, marked. Not from a date —
       nothing here carries one, and a date is a thing somebody has to remember
       to set. From the LIST: the addresses of the proposals that were on the
       page last time are kept, and anything not among them is new. Add one to
       trails_data.py and it marks itself, for everybody who has been here
       before and for nobody who has not.

       ── THREE MORE FILES ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
       tour-pace.js   how long a card is worth — asked by both tours
       map-tour.js    the tour of the whole network
       remembers.js   where you left off, and what is new

 18.0  THE SITE ANSWERS BACK. The biggest set of changes since the cards
       themselves, and most of it is one idea: the things on these pages that
       looked like controls should BE controls, and the ones that are should
       say so when you touch them.

       ── ON A TRAIL PAGE ──────────────────────────────────────────────────

       A TOUR BUTTON, between the two arrows. Press it and the page walks the
       trail for you: it scrolls, it opens each waypoint's card, it turns that
       card's pages and its pictures, it closes it and it moves on. Escape
       leaves it, so does pressing the button again, and so does touching the
       wheel — at which point you have taken over and a page that keeps
       driving itself is fighting you. A line at the foot of the screen says so
       while it is running.

       NOTHING ABOUT THE PACE IS WRITTEN DOWN ANYWHERE. The words on each card
       are counted and divided by a reading speed; the pictures each ask for a
       look, and a video asks for its own length; the longer of the two is how
       long that card is worth. Then both tracks are laid out so the last page
       of text and the last photograph arrive together, and the time from there
       to the end is the last page's turn to be read. Add a waypoint next year
       with four more photographs and twice the text and it is paced correctly
       without a number changing. The one lever worth knowing is
       `wordsPerMinute` at the top of autoplay.js — it is 200, which is a
       careful read rather than a skim.

       THE ELEVATION GRAPH IS NOT A PICTURE ANY MORE. Point at a waypoint and
       its dot swells and says its name — the names were always there, in a
       `title` attribute, which is to say they were there for nobody. Click the
       name and the page goes there and opens that card. And the whole strip is
       a scrubber: drag it and the trail runs past under your hand, the map
       following. The cards stay shut while you drag, because thirteen of them
       flashing open and closed in two seconds is not information.

       A CARD NOW COMES OUT OF ITS OWN WAYPOINT. The circle you clicked and the
       panel that answers are the same thing, and a card that faded up
       somewhere else never said so.

       CLICKING A WAYPOINT IS A TOGGLE. The one whose card is up puts it away;
       any other swaps to it — and now actually swaps to it. Clicking a distant
       waypoint used to do nothing at all: the card opened and the walk, still
       standing where it was, took it back in the same frame. It stays until
       you scroll, and then cards go back to opening and closing by themselves.
       The waypoint being read is ringed on the map, so the second click has
       something to aim at.

       ESCAPE SHUTS EVERYTHING. An expanded card shrinks first; Escape again
       leaves you looking at the trail.

       ── ON THE MAIN MAP ──────────────────────────────────────────────────

       A PANEL GREETS SOMEBODY THE FIRST TIME THEY OPEN IT, and only the first
       time. It is the trail pages' opening box with two things added: a slow
       four-colour wash in the map's own inks — a built trail, a wishful one,
       the labels, the rivers — and a purple line that draws itself round the
       edge and takes the panel away when it closes the circuit. The line is a
       clock you can read without reading anything: the panel is not asking you
       to wait, it is showing you how long it means to keep you. Continue sends
       it away at once, and it leaves the way a panel should — swelling before
       it goes rather than being switched off.

       ── EVERYWHERE ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────

       THE COIN TURNS OVER AND THE NEEDLE SWINGS UP. Both of them are readings
       rather than decoration — the coin says what a connection would cost, the
       dial says how badly it is wanted — and a reading that arrives is read
       where one that is simply there is scenery. Every time either appears,
       not once per page: a card that opens, closes and opens again does it
       again, because the second time is the first time for whoever is looking.

       The needle swings up from the bottom of the scale to whatever angle it
       is drawn at, and goes a little past before it settles. It reads the
       angle off the drawing rather than being told it, so a fourth reading
       added to the dial one day swings correctly with nothing changed.

       THE COMPASS WOBBLES WHEN YOU POINT AT IT, the way a knocked needle does.
       It is the one thing on the site that moves purely because it is nice,
       and it earns that by being the one part of the interface that is an
       instrument.

       ── THE COMING SOON PAGE ─────────────────────────────────────────────

       THE CURSOR PUSHES INSTEAD OF BLOCKING. The circle round it was ground
       closed to every line, and a wall has one behaviour a map must never
       have: a line that meets one STOPS. Sweeping the mouse across the picture
       was killing whatever it touched, and the soft disc of page colour that
       went with it read as a hole burned in the artwork. Both are gone. A line
       within reach of the cursor now keeps drawing and simply takes whichever
       way leads furthest from the middle — it curves out and carries on.

       AND A CLICK PLANTS A ROUTE, of a kind picked at random: a trail, an
       unpaved one, one under construction, a bike lane, a wishful one. It
       starts under the cursor and the push is what sends it away, so it
       appears to run out from under your own finger. Two new kinds were added
       to the picture for it, both of them out of the legend as everything else
       is: unpaved is a trail's width in a duller green, under construction is
       a trail's width in dots.

       THE PICTURE NEVER REFUSES A CLICK — refusing one feels like a fault — so
       instead it clears up faster. Past a comfortable number of lines
       everything starts rubbing itself out more quickly, more so the fuller it
       gets, and past a hard ceiling the oldest line is retired to make room.
       Somebody hammering the mouse gets a busy picture that empties as fast as
       they fill it, and never a seized one.

       ── AND FOUR NEW FILES ───────────────────────────────────────────────
       autoplay.js      the tour
       trail-graph.js   the elevation graph's hovering, clicking and dragging
       first-hello.js   the map's greeting
       icon-life.js     the coin and the needle

       None of the first three is needed to read a page and none can break one:
       each waits for the engine to say it is ready and reaches it only through
       window.TRAIL. Delete any one <script> tag and that page loses that one
       feature and nothing else.

 17.4  WHERE THE CURSOR IS, THE MAP IS NOT. The drawing answers to the pointer
       now, and it is deliberately not a control: nothing lights up, nothing is
       drawn for the cursor, there is no instruction anywhere and nothing to
       click. A circle of ground around the pointer is simply closed to every
       line, the way a golf course is, so the drawing parts as you move through
       it and closes up again behind you. Leave the mouse still and it settles
       into a real clearing — trails and roads route around it and it empties
       out. Take the pointer off the picture and it fills back in.

       TWO THINGS MAKE IT, and it needs both.

       The rule stops a line GROWING into the circle, and leans a line that has
       to turn towards the way that takes it further from the cursor, so it
       bends away in advance rather than running up against an invisible edge
       and stopping. On its own that is a swerve you are not sure you saw:
       everything already drawn stays exactly where it was.

       So whatever IS already inside the circle is faded back into the page as
       well — solid at the middle, easing back to full strength by the edge. No
       ring, no rim, no spotlight. What that buys, besides being visible, is
       that a line which has routed around the clearing comes back into view at
       full strength exactly where the fade has finished, so nothing ever looks
       cut off.

       On a phone the clearing is capped at about a quarter of the short side
       of the picture: five cells is judged against a wide screen, and on a
       small one the same five cells would be a hole half the width of it.

       And the canvas takes pointer events at all now, which it did not — it
       was marked as a picture and nothing else, back when that is all it was.
       It is still not a control in every other respect: nothing to select,
       nothing to drag, no offer to save an image on a long press, and a double
       tap does not zoom the page.

       NOBODY WHO HAS ASKED FOR LESS MOVEMENT GETS ANY OF THIS. They get the
       one still drawing they always got, with no loop behind it — a picture
       that quietly rearranged itself under the cursor is exactly the thing
       they turned off.

 17.3  NOTHING IN THE DRAWING RUNS ON TOP OF ANYTHING ELSE. Only the trails
       used to claim the ground they stood on, so a bike lane could be laid
       down the middle of an expressway and a railway could run along a river,
       and now and then one did.

       Every line of every kind now claims each STEP it takes, and no other
       line may take that same step. Which is the whole rule, because the
       difference between crossing and overlapping is exactly one thing —
       whether two lines share an EDGE of the grid or only a corner of it. One
       going east through a point and one going north through it use different
       edges: that is a crossing, at ninety degrees, and it is allowed and
       encouraged. Two lines using the same edge are running along each other,
       and that is refused.

       The key is the middle of the step with the step's ANGLE written after
       it. The middle on its own is not enough: the two diagonals of a square
       share their middle, and those two are a perfectly good ninety-degree
       crossing.

       AND THE SHORT GREY LINES ARE GONE, both sorts of them.

       The first sort was a piece of road painted back across a trail to say
       the trail went UNDER it. On the picture that read as a stray grey dash
       lying on a green line rather than as a road passing beneath. Crossings
       are now either bridges — the bright green dot, as before — or level
       crossings, which get no mark at all, which is how the map draws them.

       The second sort was the last inch of a line before its tail caught its
       head: a stub with no beginning and no end. A line now fades out over its
       last two cells rather than being drawn down to a nub, and a line that
       stopped almost as soon as it started is whipped off the picture instead
       of sitting there.

       Also: a diagonal step could clip the corner of a golf course, because
       only the end and the middle of a step were tested against one. The whole
       step is tested now.

 17.2  THE DRAWING IS THE LEGEND'S OWN LINES NOW, not lines that looked about
       right. Every width in it is the number out of the legend's own SVG,
       where everything is drawn against a trail sixteen units wide: a bike
       lane is eight of those, an expressway is sixteen with a two-unit dashed
       tan line down it, a railway is sixteen with a two-unit rail and
       eight-unit sleepers over the top, a connection is eight. One setting —
       `pen` — says what a unit is worth in pixels, so the whole picture can be
       made heavier or lighter in one place and still agree with the map.

       THE TRAILS ARE THICKER, which is what the sixteen units come to, and the
       dots are sized from the same scale — the map draws all of them at twelve
       units on a sixteen-unit trail, so they now sit inside the line with a
       rim of green all the way round instead of straddling its edges.

       A BIKE LANE IS DOTTED RATHER THAN DASHED, which is how the map draws a
       green line that is not a finished trail.

       THREE NEW THINGS ON THE PICTURE
         connections     grey, and they no longer wander off into nothing. A
                         connection is given a real place on one trail and a
                         real place on ANOTHER, and steers for it — so it
                         arrives, it gets a white junction dot at both ends,
                         and it sits there a moment before it goes. That is
                         what the short grey lines were trying to be.
         dangerous connections   the same thing in red.
         rivers          the legend's own two-tone channel, blue water between
                         dark banks, running under everything else. A trail
                         crossing one always crosses OVER it — that is what a
                         bridge is — so it always gets the bright green dot.

       THE EXPRESSWAYS AND RAILWAYS RUN MUCH FURTHER, nearly twice as far, and
       hardly turn at all. They are what the trails are crossing; a short one
       going nowhere looked like a mistake.

       THE TRAILS RUN FURTHER TOO — half as long again — and branch more often,
       up to twice each rather than once.

       AND FOUR RULES THAT MAKE IT READ AS A MAP RATHER THAN AS A TANGLE
         · NOTHING RUNS ALONGSIDE ITS OWN KIND. Every step leaves a soft mark,
           and no line of that kind may step within `apart` cells of somebody
           else's. A line may ignore its own marks — but only the last few
           steps of them, so it cannot come back and run beside a stretch of
           itself either.
         · NO HAIRPINS. A turn goes the opposite way from the last one unless
           the dice say otherwise, which they rarely do. Two turns the same way
           is a line folding back on itself, and that was most of what looked
           wrong.
         · NO FRAME. A line stopped by the edge used to find that running ALONG
           the edge was as roomy as anything else, and the picture grew a
           border. Room is now measured a whole leg ahead, where running along
           the edge is still jammed against it, so the line comes back inside.
         · A LINE THAT KEEPS BEING BLOCKED STOPS. It is in a corner it cannot
           get out of, and left trying it ties itself into a knot of short
           zigzags. Better that it ends and something starts somewhere emptier.

       Forty-five degree legs are kept short, so a diagonal reads as the map's
       way of getting round something rather than as a mountain drawn across
       the picture. Sixty frames a second, and flat memory over a minute.

 17.1  THE BENDS IN THE DRAWING ARE CURVES NOW, not cut corners. Where a trail
       turned, it used to have the point sliced off it — two marks a little way
       back along each leg with one short straight step between them. At the
       width these lines are drawn that reads as a chamfer, not as a bend.

       Each turn is now a quadratic curve that leans on the corner it replaces:
       it leaves the first leg going exactly the way that leg was going, arrives
       on the second going exactly the way that one goes, and bows through the
       middle. It is carried as six short straight pieces across a right angle
       and fewer across a shallower one, each of them shorter than the line is
       wide, so the rounded joins between them close up and what you see is a
       curve — while everything that measures DISTANCE ALONG A LINE (how much
       of it is drawn, where a dot sits, where a road crosses it) goes on
       working the way it always did, with no arc lengths to solve.

       The radius went up with it, from 9 to 14, because a curve can afford to
       start further back than a chamfer could. Both numbers are in SETTINGS at
       the top of coming-soon.js: `corner` for how far back a bend begins and
       `cornerSteps` for how finely a right angle is cut.

 17.0  THE COMING SOON PAGE IS TWO WORDS AND A DRAWING. It is NOT in the
       navigation bar on any page, and was not meant to be: it is somewhere to
       send people when a link is not ready yet, so the only way to it is a
       link you hand out. The text has gone; what is left is "Coming soon" in
       big letters and the picture, full width, straight onto the page in the
       page's own colour with no panel and no border around it. One screen, no
       scrolling.

       AND THE PICTURE IS A MAP NOW RATHER THAN A TANGLE.

       It runs at half the speed. Trails go horizontally and vertically, with
       only the occasional forty-five degree leg, and they turn on a radius the
       way the map's do rather than on a sharp point.

       NO TRAIL CROSSES ANOTHER TRAIL, OR ITSELF. Every step a trail takes
       claims the ground under it, and nothing else may step there until the
       far end has been rubbed out and given it back — the rule a game of snake
       plays by. They still cross expressways and railways freely, and they
       still will not cross a golf course, which is a red SQUARE now rather
       than whatever shape the dice gave it.

       WHAT THE DOTS MEAN, all of them the map's own marks, and all of them
       popping in rather than appearing:
         white       a trail has branched here
         bright green  the trail crosses OVER a road or a railway. Where it
                     goes under there is no dot, and the road is drawn back
                     across the trail so you can see it pass beneath.
         red         a pinch point. Never more than three at once, and each
                     one goes again after five to seven seconds.

       And every so often a purple line — something proposed rather than built
       — draws itself in and straight back out again at more than twice the
       speed.

       Sixty frames a second at every window size tested, on a phone included.

 16.0  A NEW PAGE — COMING SOON — with a moving picture on it. A trail network
       draws itself across a panel in the map's own colours and its own
       geometry: green trails running straight or at forty-five degrees,
       forking, with white dots where they meet and where they end; bike lanes
       dashed in and out; expressways and railways underneath; red blocks where
       a golf course is in the way, which the trails turn to get around. When a
       line has run as long as it is allowed, its far end starts rubbing itself
       out and it travels across the picture. There are no labels — it is a map
       of nowhere.

       It is drawn by coming-soon.js, on a canvas, with no image file. Every
       number in it is in the SETTINGS block at the top of that file: how many

       a second on a phone, and it stops running altogether when the tab is

       THE WAYPOINT BADGES ARE HALF AGAIN AS BIG and much harder to miss. They

 12.0  HEIGHTS CAN NOW BE READ STRAIGHT OFF THE DRAWING. A route may carry
       HEIGHT MARKS: small dots placed along it and NAMED for the height at
       that point — `h-118` is 118 metres above sea level. One every 500
       metres. The profile is built from them and from the waypoints together,
       so a trail with three waypoint cards can still have an honest elevation
       graph: the valley between two stops exists, instead of the straight line
       that used to be drawn through it.

       They are heights and nothing else. No card, no name chip, no dot on the
       elevation graph, and nothing visible on the page — the stylesheet hides
       anything in the artwork whose name begins with h-, and the engine hides
       them again as it reads them. Where a mark sits on top of a waypoint the
       waypoint wins, because somebody typed that number and its card shows it.

       EVERY MAP THIS BUILD DRAWS NOW HAS THEM, at the height the waypoints
       imply — which changes nothing until one is corrected, and that is the
       point. They are a scaffold: open the map in Illustrator, rename the dot
       at the bottom of the ravine to what it really is, and the trail page
       follows. `h-118`, `h-118_1` (what Illustrator writes when two objects
       share a name) and `h-118-2` all mean 118.

       AND THE TRAILS PAGE'S GRAPHS READ AS MEASUREMENTS. A faint kilometre
       grid behind each one — every kilometre up to 12 km, every two beyond,
       with every fifth line a shade brighter — and the climb end to end
       written small in the corner: −75 m, +40 m. The build refuses to publish
       a page whose figure has drifted from the heights it was drawn from.

 11.0  THE TRAILS PAGE SORTS ITSELF, four ways, and every card now carries the
       shape of its climb.

       BY TYPE is the page as it was — railpaths, ravines, the waterfront, the
       hydro corridors, and the trails that do not exist yet at the end — and
       it is what the page opens on. It is also the only reading that groups:
       every other one is a single ranked list, so the group headings go while
       it is on and the lone waterfront card stops taking the full width.

       SHORTEST FIRST brings a slider down with it: a maximum length, which
       cannot go below the shortest trail on the map, so the list can never be
       emptied. Choosing any other reading closes the slider AND clears it — a
       filter nobody can see is a filter nobody remembers is on.

       MOST POPULAR sorts on a number written on each card and shown nowhere:
       1 is the busiest trail on the map, 10 the quietest. A trail with no
       figure — the two wishful ones, and any future trail before somebody
       works it out — sorts to the end rather than the top.

       FLATTEST FIRST sorts on metres of climb and descent per kilometre,
       worked out from the heights of that trail's waypoints. That figure is
       the one thing the page shows only while it is being sorted on: on any
       other reading it is a number in the way.

       AND EVERY CARD HAS ITS ELEVATION GRAPH, under the picture, two thirds
       of its height. It is the strip from the foot of a trail page with
       everything that only makes sense while walking taken out — no cursor,
       no waypoint dots, no walked line. EVERY GRAPH ON THE PAGE IS DRAWN TO
       ONE SCALE, 20 m to 190 m, which covers every trail: drawn to its own
       scale each would fill its box and the flattest trail in the city would
       look like the steepest. On one scale the Waterfront is a flat line low
       down, Finch a gentle rise high up, and the flatness sort shows you
       something.

       Nothing is ever moved in the document — the order is a CSS `order` —
       so a card keeps its picture, its focus and its place under the pointer
       however the list is arranged. And the build now refuses to publish a
       trails page whose numbers have drifted from the trails themselves: the
       length, the popularity, the hilliness and the number of points in each
       graph are all checked against the trail data before anything goes out.

 10.2  THE CAPTION IS IN THE PLACE THAT SUITS THE CARD IT IS ON. In the small
       card it is back below the picture and beside the title, exactly where it
       was in 10.0 — the shared line, the top alignment, and the rule that a
       title too long to sit beside it drops underneath rather than pushing it
       away. Only the expanded card writes it across the photograph.
       The words are written into the card twice and the stylesheet shows
       whichever copy belongs; the engine fills both every time a picture
       changes, so the hidden one can never go stale.

       And on the photograph it now sits on the bottom edge rather than a line
       above it. It was being held clear of the row of dots, which cost the
       corner a line on every card to avoid a collision that needs both a very
       long caption and a very narrow picture to happen at all — and in the
       expanded view the picture is never narrow.

 10.1  THE PICTURE'S CAPTION IS ON THE PICTURE, in the bottom right corner,
       dressed like the waypoint number in the top left: a shadow under the
       letters and a wash of dark behind them, so it reads over a bright sky
       as easily as over a dark one. On a card with several pictures it sits
       one line higher, clear of the row of dots.
       Everything that used to keep it and the waypoint title from colliding
       is gone with it — the shared line, the reserved width for the longest
       caption a card might show, the rule about which of the two drops. A
       caption on the thing it describes needs none of it, and the words below
       get the line back.

       THE BIG CARD IS LAID OUT LIKE THE SMALL ONE: title at the top of the
       panel, elevation and distance along the bottom, and the text filling
       everything between. It used to centre the three as one block, which
       left a band of empty panel under the facts and gave the words less room
       than the card actually had. The title stops short of the two buttons in
       the corner rather than the whole panel being pushed down past them,
       which cost a line on every card to protect the two or three with a
       title long enough to reach.

       A LINK TO ONE WAYPOINT NO LONGER SHOWS YOU THE TRAIL IT IS ON. Following
       a proposal from the Wishful thinking page meant watching the trail open
       at its beginning and travel to the waypoint, because the page cannot
       honour the address until it has built its map and measured its own
       height. Now a plain cover with a turning ring is held over that, and the
       page underneath is set standing at the waypoint rather than walking to
       it. The cover comes down when there is something worth looking at — and
       after seven seconds regardless, because a cover that outlives what it is
       covering is worse than no cover.

 10.0  A WAYPOINT CARD CAN BE OPENED BIG. A second button in the card's top
       corner, beside the ×, puts that one waypoint in the middle of the
       screen at about three times the size, over a veil, with the walk held
       still behind it. Press it again, press Escape, click the veil or close
       the card and everything goes back.

       IT TURNS ON ITS SIDE TO DO IT. A card with its picture above its words
       cannot be made bigger on a normal screen — the height runs out first,
       and a 4:3 picture wide enough to be worth looking at is taller than the
       window once the text is under it. Laid out sideways, the height that was
       being spent on words goes to the picture and the words take the space
       beside it, which is where a wide screen has room going spare. The
       picture keeps its 4:3 exactly (the card's height is set to whatever
       makes that true) and goes from 538x301 to 844x632 on a 1440 screen —
       3.3 times the area. Most waypoints stop being paged at all: three pages
       of text become one.

       THE PAGE DOES NOT SCROLL WHILE IT IS OPEN. Scrolling this page is
       walking the trail, and the smallest scroll would carry the card being
       read off the screen. The wheel, the scrolling keys and anything else are
       all refused — but the page is NOT given overflow:hidden, which takes the
       scrollbar away and makes the map jump fifteen pixels sideways at the
       exact moment the card is trying to move smoothly.

       THE CHANGE IS ANIMATED, not snapped: the contents fade out over 130ms,
       the shape changes and the text is re-broken into pages while there is
       nothing to see, and the panel itself travels between the two sizes over
       260ms with the words coming back as it arrives. Somebody who has asked
       their computer for less movement gets the bigger card at once instead.

       DESKTOP ONLY, and not a small desktop window either: under 1100px wide
       or 620px tall the picture and the words cannot sit side by side and the
       big view would be no improvement, so the button is not offered.

       A CARD ALWAYS OPENS SMALL — closing one puts the view back, so the next
       card to appear is the ordinary one beside the map. The exception is a
       proposal arrived at from the Wishful thinking page: that is a link to
       one particular idea, and it opens showing it properly.

  9.2  THE CAPTION NEVER DROPS BELOW THE TITLE. When a waypoint title and the
       picture's caption are too long to share a line — "Bridge over Dufferin
       St." beside "The trail through the houses" — it is the TITLE that moves
       down onto a line of its own, and the caption keeps the corner under the
       photograph it belongs to. Before, the caption fell underneath the title
       and out to the left, which read as a stray line of text.
       They are also never allowed to finish closer than --caption-gap (26px)
       apart. Two pixels between them was what made the Dufferin card look like
       one run-on line; anything tighter than the gap now breaks instead.
       And the room kept for the caption is measured from the LONGEST caption
       on that card, not the one showing, so paging through a card's pictures
       never jerks the title down a line and back up again.

  9.1  The caption is aligned with the TOP of the waypoint title rather than
       its baseline, and lifted three pixels further still, so it sits close
       under the bottom edge of the photograph and reads as a note on the
       picture rather than a footnote to the heading. Sixteen pixels higher
       than it was.
       The title has not moved: the lift is a negative margin on the caption
       alone, so the card holds the same ten lines of text it did before. The
       distance is --caption-lift in the stylesheet's variable block.

  9.0  The picture's caption is back below the picture and over to the right,
       where it was. The waypoint number stays stamped on the photograph.
       It shares the title's line rather than having a row of its own, so it
       costs nothing on a wide card: the title takes what it needs and the
       caption takes what is left. A caption long enough to squeeze the title
       wraps underneath instead — a heading cut to two words so that a note can
       stay beside it is the wrong way round.
       Ten lines of text in one page on a desktop, unchanged. On a phone the
       caption's line costs one of the six, which is the honest price of having
       it back.
  8.9  THE CARD IS MOSTLY WORDS NOW. On a 1400x900 screen a stop showed three
       lines over four pages two versions ago, eight over two pages last
       version, and TEN IN ONE PAGE here — the Allan Rd entry is read straight
       through with no arrows to press. The picture is bigger than it was, not
       smaller.
       The waypoint number and the picture's caption moved onto the top left of
       the photograph. They were a row between the picture and the title, which
       cost 21 pixels and put two small grey things between a photograph and
       the heading that names it. Over the picture they cost nothing, and the
       TITLE NOW BEGINS THE MOMENT THE PICTURE ENDS. Both carry a shadow and a
       soft darkening under them, because over a photograph nothing can be
       relied on for contrast.
       Cost and priority moved into the row of figures, after From start. They
       had a panel of their own beside the title, which made them the loudest
       thing on the card; they are the third and fourth facts about a stop and
       now read as that. No border — the row already has a rule above it, and a
       box inside a box is one box too many.
       "Scroll for more" is gone. It said, at the foot of every card, that the
       page was still a scroll: worth saying once, not thirteen times, and it
       cost a row of the thing the card is shortest of.
       The paragraph text is a point smaller (15px to 14px), which is another
       line and a half in the same card.
       ON A PHONE the cap on how many lines may show was still 4, set when the
       card's chrome took a hundred pixels more than it does — so the card was
       finishing 52 pixels short of the readouts with the text still cut into
       four pages, because the cap and not the room was the limit. It is 7 now,
       and a stop reads in three pages rather than five.
       The stepper arrows line up with the card's own left edge rather than the
       screen's, and the card holds a clear strip at its foot for them. The
       scroll hint used to be that strip.
  8.8  A trail page's layer switches are one per row at every size.
       They were in two columns, which is right for the main map's panel — a
       dozen switches that would otherwise run off the bottom of a phone — and
       wrong for a trail page, which has two or three. Two columns made them a
       pair of narrow boxes with their names broken across lines, side by side,
       for a panel that was no shorter for it.
       The notes under each name come back on a phone as a result. They were
       dropped there as the price of fitting the main map's dozen into two
       columns; a trail page has the room now, and it needs them — "Tap a
       waypoint to open it" is the only place that says what Waypoint auto-load
       does. A phone on its side still loses them: there is no height to spare.
  8.7  MORE ROOM FOR THE WORDS, and the pictures the same size.
       A card on a 1400x900 screen was showing THREE lines of text at a time —
       the minimum the engine will go to — and turning a stop's words into four
       pages. It shows eight now, in two pages, and the photograph is one pixel
       shorter than it was. The room came from three places:
         · the card column started below the corner title even on a wide screen
           where the two cannot touch — the title ends around x=330 and the card
           is capped at 540px against the right edge. Past 1000px it now starts
           near the top of the map, which is 80 pixels.
         · the scroll hint had a rule above it, twelve pixels of air either side
           and a line of its own, costing 41 pixels — nearly two lines of the
           text it sat under. It has no rule and much less air; the facts row
           above it already draws a line, and a second one four millimetres
           below the first was drawing a box around nothing.
         · a few pixels each off the paddings, the title's margin, the pager and
           the facts row.
       --card-text-lines went from 7 to 10, or the extra room would have been
       left empty on a tall screen.
       LINKS WORK INSIDE A STOP'S TEXT, and the Beltline has four of them as
       worked examples. Underlined rather than only coloured, because over a
       photograph the card's background is not reliably dark; a link leaving the
       site gets a small ↗ drawn in the text, so it prints and scales.
       WAYPOINT AUTO-LOAD is a new switch in the layers panel, on by default.
       Off, nothing opens by itself and a stop opens when you tap it — which is
       the better way to LOOK at the map, since nothing covers it unless you ask.
       It is one term in one expression in the drawing loop, because everything
       about a card was already written in terms of "how much should this be
       showing".
       THE STEPPER ARROWS ARE VISIBLE ON A PHONE. The card is full width there
       and is deliberately laid over the rail, which buried the one control that
       is needed exactly when a card is up — greyed and unreachable for the whole
       walk. The arrows now sit above the card in its bottom-left corner, and the
       scroll hint has moved to the right to give them the lane. Eighteen pixels,
       which is the whole price of having both.
  8.6  The Wishful thinking page stops moving when it should not.
       THE SCROLLBAR NO LONGER SHIFTS THE PAGE. Filtering twelve cards down to
       one made the page short enough not to need a scrollbar, the scrollbar
       went away, and on Windows and Linux the whole layout jumped fifteen
       pixels sideways — everything moving at once, none of it for a reason.
       The reading pages now reserve the gutter whether or not they need it
       (scrollbar-gutter: stable, with the older always-show-the-track trick
       for browsers that do not know the property). The map pages do not: they
       never scroll, and a permanent gutter beside a full-bleed map is a stripe
       of nothing down the edge of the artwork.
       CARDS ARRIVE AND LEAVE PROPERLY. A card that stops matching now shrinks
       and fades for 150ms and is taken out of the layout after that, rather
       than blinking out and letting the ones behind it jump into the hole.
       Arriving is slower than leaving on purpose: you are waiting for what is
       left, not watching what has gone.
       The filtering also stopped doing sixty times the work it needed to. The
       sliders call it on every frame while they are being dragged, and almost
       every one of those frames asks for the same set of cards; it now
       compares what was asked for against what is already shown and returns
       early, so an animation can finish instead of restarting each frame.
  8.5  THE BELTLINE IS NO LONGER A SPECIAL CASE. It was a hand-written page —
       the same page as the other eleven, but maintained separately, so every
       change to the shared template had to be made twice and its three wishful
       stops had to be listed a second time for the Wishful thinking page to
       find them. There was a check that refused to build if those two lists
       disagreed; the check worked, and it was a symptom. A list that has to be
       guarded against itself should not be two lists.
       Everything that makes the Beltline itself — its words, its photographs,
       its videos, its text, its heights — is in trails_data.py now, and
       make_pages.py writes its page exactly as it writes the others: where a
       trail supplies real content the generator uses it, and where it does not
       the generator invents a placeholder. The regenerated page was checked
       against the hand-written one waypoint by waypoint, including every
       caption and every measured position along the route, and is identical.
       Two things about it still differ and both are now stated in its entry
       rather than assumed in four scripts: artwork="beltline" (a railpath is
       not a river, so make_beltline.py draws it) and real=True.
       DO NOT EDIT beltline.html. Edit its entry in trails_data.py.
       On a waypoint card, cost and priority moved from a band of their own to
       the title's line, smaller, on a panel lighter than the card rather than
       darker — as a band they were the loudest thing on the card, and a dark
